# Prime Factorisation and Divisors

Every integer greater than one has a unique prime factorisation apart from factor order. Trial division repeatedly removes the smallest available factor. After testing divisors only up to the square root of the remaining value, any leftover value greater than one must itself be prime. Notice that the loop bound should depend on the shrinking remainder, not the original number; this can substantially reduce work.

If `n = p1^e1 * p2^e2 * ...`, every divisor chooses an exponent between zero and `ei` for each prime. Therefore the divisor count is `(e1+1)(e2+1)...`, and divisors can be generated by repeatedly multiplying an accumulating list by each permitted prime power. This is better than scanning from `1` to `n`, especially when `n` is large but has few divisors.

The problem asks for a full factor profile: prime factors with exponents, sorted divisors, divisor count and divisor sum. The divisor-sum formula is the product of geometric sums `1+p+...+p^e`, though direct generation can also produce the sum. A strong solution factors once and reuses the result; factoring separately for every requested property repeats expensive work.

Tests include primes, prime powers, products of distinct primes and a large semiprime. Expected factoring time is `O(sqrt n)` worst case, divisor generation is `O(tau(n))`, and output space is necessarily proportional to the number of divisors.
